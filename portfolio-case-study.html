Disease Burden Forecasting & Geographic Risk Modelling

Where reported disease concentrates—and how uncertain the outlook is. A West Nile surveillance workspace connecting area-level rates, empirical-Bayes modelling, historical forecasts and transparent planning evidence.

CDC ArboNETCensus population estimatesPython · SQLIndependent temporal test

The planning question

Which geographies deserve closer review, and what can historical patterns actually tell us?

Reported case counts describe operational burden. Population-adjusted rates describe a different aspect of geographic exposure to reported disease. Forecasts add an outlook, but must be evaluated before they are used in a planning conversation.

This project combines CDC West Nile surveillance with matching-year Census populations. State briefings expose all three views without treating reported cases as total infections or area-level patterns as individual risk.

Data discipline

Geographic comparisons need defensible denominators.

The expanded analytical panel contains 2,244 category/state/year observations for 2004–2025. The CDC map file records positive-count entries; omitted entries become zero reported cases only after every category/year state total reconciles with independently downloaded national monthly totals.

Reported rates are available only for 2020–2025, when matching-year population denominators are included. Neuroinvasive disease is analysed as a subset of all disease; the two categories are never summed. Puerto Rico is excluded from the 51-unit analytical scope.

Geographic rate uncertainty

A high reported rate is a starting point for review, not an individual risk prediction.

A Gamma-Poisson empirical-Bayes model shares information across state-level reported rates. Posterior means and conditional 90% credible ranges make small-count uncertainty visible. Geographic profiles also compare current rates with recent baselines.

The model does not incorporate adjacency, transmission, underdiagnosis or hyperparameter uncertainty. It supports area-level interpretation; it is not a clinical risk score, formal hotspot test or causal explanation.

Chronological design

Selection, calibration and final testing remain separate.

Origins in 2011–2012 select the global model separately for each category, using targets through 2015. Origins in 2015–2017 calibrate uncertainty using targets through 2020. A fixed origin in 2022 produces held-out forecasts for 2023–2025.

Every forecast uses only data through its origin. Latest-vintage historical revisions still mean this is a retrospective chronological test, not a reconstruction of the files that were available in real time.

Forecast evidence

A selected model does not automatically win on later data.

The five-year mean wins earlier selection for both disease categories. On the final neuroinvasive test, it records 21.04 cases of mean absolute error per state/year; last-year persistence records 18.95. The test winner is reported without retrospectively replacing the selected method.

Empirical forecast bands show 88.2%, 96.1% and 96.1% coverage across the 51 units at horizons one, two and three for neuroinvasive disease. These are observed results, not a guaranteed coverage level. Calibration windows and geographic units are dependent.

Usable evidence

From national patterns to a downloadable state briefing.

The application provides maps, state histories, population-adjusted comparisons, area-level uncertainty, a forecast laboratory and downloadable CSV evidence. The 2026–2028 planning outlook is clearly separated from reported history and the held-out test.

Python produces the analytical panel and model evidence. SQLite stores the tables, and executed SQL summarises regional reported burden. Committed outputs make the application runnable without credentials or live data access.

Limits & sources

Reported disease is not the whole burden of infection.

Non-neuroinvasive illness is particularly underreported. Residence may differ from exposure location. Climate, mosquito surveillance, interventions and local reporting practices are not modelled; annual forecasts cannot function as weekly outbreak warnings. Future incidence rates are not fabricated from unknown population denominators.
